It is easy to begin IELTS Speaking practice by looking for more and more materials. A more useful beginning is usually simpler: answer one question, listen back to yourself, and improve only one thing in the next attempt. This plan does not give an official score. It helps you notice your own speech and make the next attempt more deliberate.

## Build a small system first

Three or four short sessions of 20–30 minutes each week are enough. Use a phone recorder or the materials in the [IELTS Speaking course](/en/kurslar/ielts-speaking/). Keep three simple note sections: “question,” “what happened,” and “next attempt.” A complicated spreadsheet is not necessary.

Do not look for a different topic every day. Returning to one topic twice is often more useful. Your first answer is likely to be natural; your second can respond to what you noticed. For example, answer a question about free time for one minute. Listen back and mark repeated words. On the second attempt, do not replace the whole idea; find clearer alternatives only for those repeated words.

## Monday: short answers

Choose six Part 1-style questions. Speak for 30–45 seconds on each. The goal is not to insert a difficult expression. The goal is to answer immediately, then add one reason or a small example.

After recording, look for four things:

- Did I answer the question in the first sentence?
- Where did fillers such as “um” or “like,” or long pauses, increase?
- Which example made my point clearer?
- Which word or idea stopped my flow?

This list is not for criticising yourself. It helps you choose one task for the next practice. For example: “Tomorrow I will not try to speak longer; I will simply finish every answer with a reason.” A small target like that is easier to observe than a general instruction to “speak better.”

## Wednesday: develop one story

Take one Part 2 card. Spend one minute making a plan: three keywords, the order of events, and a final point. Do not write a complete script. A full script can turn speaking into memorisation, which makes an unexpected question harder.

Speak for two minutes. When you listen back, check the content map: opening, event or example, ending. If you stopped at one point, do not immediately label it an “error.” You may need a linking phrase such as “because of that,” “the main reason is,” or “afterwards.” Put three or four linkers on a separate card and test only those in the next recording.

## Friday: develop an opinion

Part 3 questions usually need a more developed answer. A simple support can help: claim, reason, example, limitation. If you are talking about online learning, state your position first, explain why, give a small example, then add that it may not suit everyone. The formula should not become mechanical; you do not need all four parts every time. It is simply support when you cannot find the next idea.

On Friday, choose only two issues from an earlier recording: one language issue and one structure issue. For instance, check verb tenses and add an example. Trying to correct pronunciation, grammar, vocabulary, and content all at once is tiring and makes it hard to see which change actually helped.

## Sunday: listen back and plan

At the end of the week, select one old recording and one new recording. Do not turn them into a competition. Write answers to three questions: where was my idea clearer, where did I sound calmer, and what will I continue next week? Then choose one topic and one language target for the next week.

If you receive an automatic practice suggestion, do not treat it as a final judgement. It can be a signal to observe, but it is not an official IELTS result. For timed practice and a format closer to a test, use [IELTS Mock](/en/ielts-mock/).

## Measure progress simply

You do not need to look for one score each week. It is still useful to place three marks next to each recording: “answered immediately,” “included an example,” and “has an ending.” After a week, you can see which mark is most often missing. If examples are rare, practise only a small example from everyday life next week. If endings are missing, try one natural closing line such as “that is why it matters to me.”

When you practise with a partner, do not ask for a band score. Ask one clarity question instead: “Where was my main point unclear?” or “Which example felt unnecessary?” That gives you feedback you can use. Your partner’s role is to be an attentive listener, not an examiner.

## Keep the plan on a difficult day

Some weeks even two minutes of speaking can feel difficult. On that day, do not abandon the plan; reduce it. Record a twenty-second answer to one question and listen once. Then it is enough to write, “I continued today.” Consistency often comes not from long sessions but from a small step that is easy to return to.

If one error repeats, turn it into a separate lesson. For example, if you mix past simple and present perfect, describe five personal events using both tenses. If finding words is difficult, do not memorise ten words for every topic; use three useful phrases in context. Reuse a new phrase in the next Part 1 and Part 2 answer to help it stay available.

This plan is useful before an exam too. Instead of searching for another method, choose three habits from your previous recordings: answer the question, give a reason, provide a small example. These simple supports work across many topics. Listening to yourself can feel uncomfortable, but it is exactly what shows which small next step can make your answer clearer.
