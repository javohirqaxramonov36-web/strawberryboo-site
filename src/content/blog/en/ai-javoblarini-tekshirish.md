AI can give you an outline, an explanation, or a first draft. A quick answer is not automatically a verified answer. The safest habit for study is to treat an AI result not as finished work, but as a draft that needs checking. The five steps below do not require specialist technical knowledge.

## 1. State the task clearly

Instead of writing “explain this topic,” state your purpose, level, format, limits, and what you need to do next. For example: “Explain the three stages of photosynthesis at a tenth-grade level; give one key term for each stage; do not invent sources.” This does not make the answer perfect, but it makes its boundary clear enough to check.

The central idea in the [Prompt Engineering course](/en/kurslar/prompt-engineering/) is similar: a useful prompt is not simply a longer prompt. It gives a clear task and a checkable result. If an answer leaves the expected format, revise the question first. Sending the same vague question several times rarely creates more reliable knowledge.

## 2. Separate the claims in the answer

Read the response and mark three kinds of sentence: a definition, a fact that can be checked, and a recommendation. You can compare a definition with a textbook. Facts such as dates, numbers, laws, admission requirements, or quotations should not be accepted without a source. A recommendation has to be tested for fit with your own situation.

For example, if AI says a university accepts a particular certificate, that is not enough evidence for a decision. Go back to the university’s official page. AI may still help you find search terms or make a first structure for a document checklist. That distinction saves time and reduces mistakes.

## 3. Go to a primary source

If AI gives a link or a name, open it. If it gives no source, search for the official page for the topic. Prefer the relevant authority for a rule, the university itself for admission requirements, and a textbook or reliable organisation for a scientific concept. A search-result headline is not evidence.

When you read a source, check three things: date, scope, and exception. Old information may not describe a current rule. A requirement for one programme may not apply to another. A sentence beginning with “usually” may hide an exception. These checks are small, but they matter most when a result affects a real decision.

## 4. Replace the example with your own

An AI example is often general. Test it with your own assignment, lesson, or plan. If the answer does not fit the information you were given, it needs revision rather than copying. This is especially important for an essay, CV, or application: another voice should not replace your real experience.

The same is true when using AI agents. Automation discussed in the [AI Agents course](/en/kurslar/ai-agentlar/) can make a process faster, but you remain the person who checks the task, the input, and the final output. Test an automated step on a small sample first. If a result is unexpected, find out whether the task, the data, or the instruction created the issue before using it at a larger scale.

## 5. Leave a short checking note

After an important AI answer, write three lines: “what was useful,” “what I verified,” and “what remains open.” This can become a personal knowledge base. Next time, you will not need to repeat the same check, and you will see which kind of task AI has actually helped you with.

Working with AI in a group needs the same care. An AI outline can help divide work, but it cannot replace agreement. First agree on the final product: a presentation, table, essay, or research question. Then compare suggested sections with the task. Remove an unnecessary section, and assign a person to any section that needs a source. This turns “AI said so” into a checkable work plan.

Do not add a citation or book title suggested by AI directly to a bibliography. Check its title, author, and publication date in a library catalogue, publisher page, or reliable academic index. If you cannot find it, do not use it. One invented or wrong reference can weaken a carefully prepared piece of work. Even when a source exists, read it to see whether it supports the specific claim you want to make.

## Small questions for quality

Finish each use with a few questions. Did the answer cover every part of my question? What facts does it rely on? Can I explain one part independently? Which sentence might affect my decision? The last question matters especially: information that changes a decision should be confirmed somewhere else.

When AI makes a mistake, you do not have to conclude that it is useless. The mistake can show that you need to improve the question, state a limit, or request a source. If an answer is too general, ask “Which criterion did you use to choose these options?” before merely asking for three more options. If it contains many assumptions, ask which parts are assumptions and which are checked. This turns you from a passive receiver into an active verifier.

Finally, rewrite the result in your own words. This might be a one-paragraph conclusion or a three-point plan. If you cannot do that, the answer may not yet be yours. The strongest habit with AI is therefore a short pause after speed: open the source, test the example, and explain the conclusion yourself. That habit will serve longer than any single new tool.

Try this process for a week. Even if you check only one response a day, by the end you may notice which questions need a source and which need a clearer plan. If you discuss the result with a teacher or colleague, they can see your checking note rather than only AI prose. That makes the thinking process visible and easier to improve.
